The Big Picture

Massachusetts Towns Are Running Out of Options

A 2025 state report found that cities and towns across Massachusetts face a historic fiscal crisis — squeezed between rising costs and strict limits on how much tax they can raise.

Source: Massachusetts Municipal Association, "A Perfect Storm" (October 2025) →

What the report found, in plain English

Researchers from Tufts University teamed up with the Massachusetts Municipal Association to study how towns and cities are doing financially. They looked at 351 communities across the state — and found a crisis hiding in plain sight.

The core problem is simple: towns can only raise property taxes 2.5% a year (that's Proposition 2½, from a 1980 ballot vote). But real costs — salaries, fuel, construction — often rise much faster, especially since COVID. And flexible state money that towns used to count on has shrunk by 25% since 2002, after adjusting for inflation.

The result: towns across Massachusetts have been quietly cutting staff, reducing services, and deferring repairs for years. Most are now so close to their legal tax limit that they have almost no room to respond on their own. For many towns, the only legal way out is a Proposition 2½ override — exactly what Easthampton is asking voters to approve on June 9.

The State Spends More. Towns Are Left Behind.

Since 2010, after adjusting for inflation, Massachusetts towns have grown their spending at just 0.6% a year. The state government grew nearly five times faster.

Source: U.S. Census Bureau, MassBudget Budget Browser, CSPA calculations. Real (inflation-adjusted) annual growth, 2010–2022.

MA Gets Less State Help Than Most States

Nationally, state governments send about 31 cents of every local government dollar. In Massachusetts it's only 26 cents.

Source: U.S. Census Bureau

Education Money Mostly Went to Big Cities

Since 2002, state education aid has grown dramatically for large gateway cities. For rural and suburban towns, it barely moved.
